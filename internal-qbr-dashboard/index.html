01 About 1 in 19 answers fails the deployment’s own quality check technical

The deployment grades its own answers, and roughly one in nineteen does not pass — improved from one in twelve at launch.

02 Model work per ticket is climbing, and we cannot say why technical

Requests grew 44.6%. The tickets they serve grew only 7.7%. Each Tier-1 ticket now arrives with 6.2 requests behind it, up from 4.6 at launch.

The climb is steady, not noise: it rose on 24 of 30 days, sits almost exactly on a straight line, survives removing both bad days, and still holds if you count only the tickets the assistant handled. The highest reading of the month is the last day of data.

There are two explanations and they point opposite ways. Either each ticket now takes more model work to resolve, or the assistant is answering questions that never become tickets at all — which is what Northstar wants. The file cannot tell us which.

03 The service broke twice this month, and both times a person had to fix it technical

The month had exactly two bad days — slower responses, more errors, less availability. Both were fixed by hand the same day; neither recovered on its own.

They are the only two days deviating more than two standard deviations from trend on error rate, response time and availability at once, under both a linear fit and a rolling median. The file names a different cause for each.

Why it matters: traffic did not cause either failure — nine later days were busier and none degraded. A content change and a capacity setting broke it, and both needed a person to catch them. Two events is a small sample, so this is a signature to watch, not a proven rule.

Competitive risk low today

Our read: low right now, and for operational reasons rather than contractual ones — nothing in the materials commits Northstar to staying.

No competitor is named anywhere in the account; all 20 columns and all three operational notes were searched. This is a judgement from what the deployment does, not from market intelligence we do not have.

Why

  • Replacing us means 3,394 tickets a day going back to Northstar's people, on top of the 1,450 they already handle. Their Tier-1 human load has already fallen 5.8% since launch — that is capacity they would need to find again.
  • A competitor has to beat a rising baseline. All 14 measures improved over the month, so whatever anyone else demos has to beat where we will be, not where we started.

What would raise it: the quality gap widening, or reaching a renewal conversation still with no service levels agreed. Both already have owners and dates on this dashboard.

What we cannot see: who else Northstar evaluated, what they pay, when they renew, or what the market is offering. Those need the account team, not the data.

The three patterns that make the case

1   It got faster while it got busier. Traffic rose 44.6% and response times fell 20% at the same time. Systems under growing load normally slow down. This one sped up, which says the platform has room to take more — the single most relevant fact for a conversation about four more brands.

2   It took work off people while taking on more work. Tier-1 volume arriving rose 7.7%, and the number of tickets still reaching a person fell 5.8%. Northstar absorbed growth without adding headcount to the Tier-1 queue.

3   Quality rose alongside volume, not at its expense. Grounding, the quality check and customer satisfaction all improved while the system handled more. The usual trade — automate more, answer worse — did not happen here.

The two cost figures we can calculate from the file

Both come straight from the file. Nothing is estimated.

1   Inference spend, as a monthly run rate

Take what the deployment cost over the last seven days of data and scale it to a 30-day month.

Spend, 25–31 Aug  =  $322.42
÷ 7 days  =  $46.06 per day
× 30 days  =  $1,382 per month

For reference, the whole 31 days cost $1,213.12. The run rate is higher than the month's average because usage grew across the month.

2   What a unit of work costs

Divide that same week’s spend by the requests it paid for. Both are metered by the deployment, so this needs no assumption about what a request was for.

Spend, 25–31 Aug  =  $322.42
÷ requests, 25–31 Aug  =  210,467
=  $1.53 per 1,000 requests

It did not move all month. $1.54 in the launch week, $1.53 in the final week, while volume grew 44.6%. Spend rose because usage rose, not because the work got dearer.

Why not cost per ticket? That needs spend divided by tickets, which assumes every request exists to serve a ticket. The file does not establish that, so the per-ticket figure is shown in the chart above with its assumption stated and is not used to price anything.
